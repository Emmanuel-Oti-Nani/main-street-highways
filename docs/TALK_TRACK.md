# Talk track: Main Street Highways

*Plain-language note: how to explain this project out loud. Every number matches the released data (v1.0.0).*

## 60 seconds

In thousands of small American towns, the main street is also a state or U.S. highway. Trucks and through traffic arrive at highway speed and share the street with people walking to shops, the post office and school. National crash reports split deaths into "urban" and "rural," so these streets disappear in between.

I built the first national dataset of them. It covers about 15,000 towns under 10,000 people in the lower 48 states. For each one I located the town center from where its shops and services cluster, then cut every state and U.S. highway into three parts: the town center, the rest of town, and the same road just outside town. I placed 39,300 fatal crashes from 2015 to 2024 on those parts.

The result is a clear trade-off. Per mile driven, fatal crashes are less common in town centers than on the approaches. But pedestrians die there at three times the rate, and more than a quarter of the people killed in town centers were walking, against about 7 percent outside town. The dataset is free and citable on Zenodo, so state DOTs and small towns can find their own main street highways and track them.

## Two sentences

I built the first national dataset of state and U.S. highways that serve as small-town main streets, with every fatal crash from 2015 to 2024 placed in the town center, the rest of town, or just outside. In town centers fatal crashes are less frequent per mile driven, but pedestrians die at three times the rate of the approaches.

## Likely questions

**Isn't that just because more people walk downtown?** Partly, yes. There is no national count of how many people walk, so the rates are per vehicle mile, not per pedestrian. The point is where deaths concentrate, and that these state-owned roads are where pedestrians are dying in small towns.

**How do you know where a town's center is?** From where its everyday destinations cluster: shops, restaurants, offices, schools and services within a quarter mile. A sample of 45 towns was checked against aerial imagery and against each town's own recognized center, such as its town hall, post office or historic district. All 45 were within a quarter mile of a post office, town hall or courthouse; in three towns that center is a highway civic or commercial strip rather than a historic downtown.

**What should an agency do with it?** Screen these segments in state safety programs, and look past the entry to town: most of these deaths are inside the downtown block, and a larger share than elsewhere happen in daylight, so crossings and speed through the center matter, not just lighting.
